This study investigated the implementation of anti-windup strategies in PID control loops, focusing on the reverse-acting temperature control of a three-state exothermic continuous stirred-tank reactor (CSTR). The cooling valve was subject to both amplitude and slew-rate constraints, and six fixed-gain PID controllers were evaluated using performance metrics based on the actual process temperature. The principal contribution is not a new anti-windup law, but a systematic analysis of how discrete implementation choices, actuator constraints, and measurement characteristics affect closed-loop performance. A computational heat-exchanger model was also considered as a second plant to assess the transferability of selected findings. Proposition~\ref{prop:latch} holds under its stated hypotheses, which were satisfied in the investigated reactor simulations but not in the heat-exchanger case. The regime map, back-calculation tracking constants, and loop-specific quantitative results are therefore specific to the investigated plants and actuator configurations. Four main conclusions are drawn, with Table~\ref{tab:conclusion_robustness} identifying the conditions under which each was evaluated.

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Anti-windup implementation details can determine the performance of a controller.}
Sign-blind amplitude clamping, which incorrectly applies the direct-acting integrator-freezing condition to a reverse-acting loop, may prevent the integrator from unwinding when required, causing the valve to remain latched at its upper limit (Proposition~\ref{prop:latch}). In the nominal reactor simulations, the temperature settles \vlatchgap{}~K below the setpoint, while the integrated absolute error (IAE) increases from \vZNdir{} to \vZNsign{}~K$\cdot$min for ZN and from \vCCdir{} to \vCCsign{}~K$\cdot$min for CC. Other implementation choices also have substantial consequences. Discretizing the measurement filter using explicit Euler and placing the backlash element before the slew-rate limiter can change the IAE by up to \vorderFourdIAE{}\% and \vorderTwodIAE{}\%, respectively, and alter the relative ranking of some controllers and anti-windup strategies. By contrast, changing the order of amplitude saturation and slew-rate limiting does not produce the same effect under the investigated conditions. These results emphasize the importance of explicitly specifying the controller implementation, filter discretization, actuator model, and ordering of constraints (Section~\ref{subsec:checklist}).

\item \textbf{The slew-rate trigger, rather than directional sign awareness alone, is essential for recovering the performance of high-gain loops under the investigated actuator constraints.}
Across three temperature steps, a load disturbance, and three reactor operating points, the rate-aware trigger improves the tracking performance of high-gain controllers. The second plant provides additional support for the relevance of this mechanism, although the results do not establish its universal effectiveness. Directional sign awareness remains a secondary factor in the cases examined. Similar behavior is observed with actuator deadband and stiction, although stiction degrades the performance of all working strategies. For actuator slew-rate limits ranging from $20$ to $320$~L/min$^2$, the trigger is beneficial for the empirical tuning rules at every tested limit and for AMIGO and Optimal ITAE when the valve response is slow. In contrast, for the slower and well-damped SIMC loop, the trigger deteriorates performance at every tested limit except the slowest. The heuristic condition $\rho \geq 1$, which compares the actuator movement demanded by the loop with the movement the valve can deliver, helps identify when the trigger is beneficial for the reactor. However, this criterion does not transfer reliably to the heat exchanger, where the trigger worsens TL performance.

\item \textbf{Under noise-free conditions, back-calculation with an appropriately selected tracking constant can achieve performance comparable to that of the directional rule.}
When one tracking constant $T_t$ is selected per control loop within $[T_d,T_i]$, based on the design step and subsequently evaluated for temperature steps of $\pm 5$~K, the worst IAE ratio relative to the directional rule ranges from \vttcvmin{} to \vttcvmax{}. For the empirical tuning rules, back-calculation also generally reduces valve travel and overshoot. In contrast, conventional default choices can produce IAE increases of up to \vttsqrtmaxpct{}\% for $T_t=\sqrt{T_iT_d}$ and \vtttimaxpct{}\% for $T_t=T_i$. Although the directional rule does not require a separately tuned tracking constant, its performance depends on the actuator slew-rate limit $S_{\max}$ and the sampling interval $\Delta t$. Furthermore, the equivalence observed under noise-free conditions does not necessarily persist in the presence of measurement noise. At a noise standard deviation of $0.05$~K, back-calculation using tracking constants selected under noise-free conditions produces performance degradation of up to \vnzbchimaxpct{}\% relative to the directional rule for the high-gain loops.

\item \textbf{The measurement path is an essential component of anti-windup implementation and performance assessment.}
The high-frequency noise-to-valve gain of the empirical tuning rules is governed by $|K_p|(1+N)$, reaching \vCSZN{} and \vCSCC{}~(L/min)/K for ZN and CC, respectively, compared with \vCSSIMC{}~(L/min)/K for SIMC. This amplification is distinct from the closed-loop sensitivity peak, which reaches only \vSlinZN{} and \vSlinCC{} for ZN and CC, respectively, when evaluated using the exact linearization. These findings demonstrate that sensitivity peaks alone are insufficient to characterize measurement-noise amplification at the actuator command. Moreover, while first-order measurement filtering consistently reduces valve travel across all tested loop--noise pairs ($\vfilttvbetter$ of $\vfiltiaen$) and narrows the back-calculation gap at moderate noise, its phase lag can increase IAE at low noise and in a subset of directional-rule cases. Consequently, measurement filtering, derivative filtering, and the treatment of actuator-position feedback should be considered explicitly when implementing and comparing anti-windup strategies.

\end{enumerate}

The accompanying code is intended to reproduce the reported tables and figures and provides a common interface for evaluating additional controllers. Future work should investigate the transferability of these findings to independently developed process models, experimentally measured valve positions, gain-scheduled controllers, and operating conditions within the open-loop unstable region.
