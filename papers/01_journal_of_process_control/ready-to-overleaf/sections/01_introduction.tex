Most process loops act through actuators bounded in stroke and in travel speed; when a controller drives such an actuator into either limit, the loop is effectively opened and the integrator winds up \cite{astrom1989windup, hippe2006windup, galeani2009tutorial, tarbouriech2009antiwindup}. PID controllers still regulate the large majority of these loops \cite{astrom2006pid, seborg2010process}, so the anti-windup logic that accompanies them is part of almost every industrial control implementation. The temperature loop of an exothermic continuous stirred tank reactor (CSTR) is a demanding instance: Arrhenius kinetics produce parametric sensitivity and multiplicity \cite{uppal1974dynamic, aris1958analysis, bequette2003process}, the loop is reverse-acting (more coolant lowers the temperature), and the cooling valve is limited in amplitude and in slew rate.

The object of study is the discrete-time implementation of the control loop: the difference equations of the integrator, the order in which amplitude, rate and backlash limits are evaluated, the discretization of the tracking and measurement-filter terms (explicit Euler against exact zero-order hold), and the signals fed back to the anti-windup logic. These choices are seldom reported: in a short audit of $\vlitcounted$ articles that compare controllers on a reactor with an actuator constraint (not a systematic review; Supplementary Section~\suppref{supp:audit}), $\vlitactuatormodel$ stated the amplitude limit but none a slew-rate limit or the action of the loop, $\vlitantiwindup$ of the $\vlitantiwindupapp$ with integral action the anti-windup logic, and $\vlitdtandmethod$ the sampling interval and the discretization. Yet in a computational benchmark these choices can decide the ranking of controllers; we show this on one reactor benchmark and check its transfer on a second plant. The failure of the reaction-curve rules of Ziegler and Nichols \cite{ziegler1942optimum} and Cohen and Coon \cite{cohen1953theoretical} on exothermic reactors is a recurring finding \cite{visioli2006practical, luyben2007chemical}, often used to motivate model-based or learning-based alternatives. This paper asks how much of such a verdict is decided by the implementation of the constraint handling rather than by the control law; it is not a PID tuning study. The gains of the six controllers used here are fixed by published rules, or by one constrained optimization, and are not designed, ranked or compared as tuning methods: they serve as a test bed that spans the range of controller aggressiveness against which the integrator logic is evaluated. Analyses that concern the tunings themselves (the optimal-ITAE design and its design-model constraint, Supplementary Section~\suppref{supp:tuning}; the derivative-filter coefficient, Table~\suppref{tab:n_design}; a global sensitivity analysis, Section~\suppref{supp:sobol}) are not used by the conclusions.

\subsection{Positioning against the anti-windup literature}
\label{subsec:positioning}
Anti-windup for PID loops is a mature topic with four families of solutions (Table~\ref{tab:aw_families}). Conditional integration (clamping) and back-calculation are the schemes implemented in practice \cite{astrom1989windup, hippe2006windup, peng1996nonlinear, bohn1995analysis}, and they are the two compared here. Compensators designed by linear matrix inequalities treat magnitude and rate saturation together \cite{galeani2009tutorial, tarbouriech2009antiwindup, lerch2021antiwindup}; they are not part of the comparison because they require a plant model and a design step outside the scope of an implementation study, whose question is what an implementation of the two industrial schemes does. Recent work refines individual schemes: rate-limited anti-windup for a fractional-order PID \cite{wang2020rate}, gradient-based optimization of anti-windup PID gains \cite{aksland2022gradient}, and learned or operator-theoretic variants of back-calculation \cite{serrano2024neuron, morohoshi2025nonlinear}. Two facts frame the present study. First, conditional integration is correctly specified by the direction of the integrator relative to the saturation (freeze when the increment pushes the command further into the stop), but it is easily implemented as the shortcut ``freeze when the command exceeds the stop and the error is positive'', which is right for a direct-acting controller only. In a reverse-acting loop ($K_p < 0$) the sign of the error that pushes the command into the stop is reversed, and a shortcut that keeps the direct-acting convention is \emph{sign-blind}. We call it \emph{sign-blind amplitude clamping} and present it as a frequent implementation error, not as a rule attributed to any publication. PID blocks of DCS and PLC libraries (standard ISA form, IEC~61131-3 languages) usually keep $K_c > 0$ and select reverse action by inverting the error at the block input, and often realize the integral action as a lag driven by external reset feedback from the actual output, which limits windup by construction \cite{shinskey1996process, astrom2006pid}. Their saturation logic thus sees a positive gain; the sign-blind error arises mainly where a loop is coded with $K_p < 0$ and the direct-acting freeze condition is copied, as in simulation scripts. Second, back-calculation depends on a tracking time constant $T_t$ that is rarely reported.

What the literature does not provide, to our knowledge, is a controlled comparison of these schemes on a reverse-acting process loop with a slew-limited valve in which the controller gains are held fixed, together with the mechanism by which the sign-blind rule fails. That is the gap addressed here. The contribution is an analysis of implementation, not a new anti-windup law.

\subsection{Contributions}
The study uses a three-state CSTR model with an explicit jacket balance and a cooling valve limited to $[0, 300]$~L/min and $80$~L/min$^2$, with error metrics evaluated on the true reactor temperature. The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A demonstration, on a reactor benchmark and a second plant, that the discrete implementation decides the verdict on a controller: sign-blind clamping, the slew-rate trigger, the tracking constant of back-calculation, explicit Euler against exact discretization of the tracking term and of the measurement filter, and the position of the backlash relative to the slew limit each change a ranking, whereas the order of saturation and slew limit does not (Sections~\ref{subsec:ablation}--\ref{subsec:hx}).
    \item A proposition that explains why sign-blind amplitude clamping latches a reverse-acting loop at full cooling, valid under its hypotheses; that the reactor reaches them is shown by simulation, not proved, and on the second plant they are not reached.
    \item A seven-policy ablation of the integrator logic, including the $2\times 2$ factorial of trigger (amplitude, or amplitude plus slew rate) and sign awareness, over three steps, a load profile, deadband, stiction and three operating points, and a regime map over slew limits of $20$ to $320$~L/min$^2$, with a rate-demand heuristic for when the slew-rate trigger helps and its counterexamples.
    \item A comparison of the directional rule with back-calculation using one tracking constant per loop, chosen by a declared criterion: equivalent without measurement noise, not with it.
    \item An account of the measurement path, in which the noise-to-valve gain is set by $|K_p|(1+N)$ rather than by a sensitivity peak, and of a measured valve position fed back to the anti-windup.
    \item A pseudocode of the integrator update and a five-item checklist for reporting the implementation of a comparison on a constrained loop (Section~\ref{subsec:checklist}).
\end{enumerate}
